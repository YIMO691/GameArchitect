Draw Call Lab

Four ways a scene gets slow. Draw calls: every slip the render thread writes for the GPU costs CPU time. Overdraw: every see-through pixel the GPU paints again costs GPU time. Culling: every object nobody can see still costs both, until you stop sending it. Level of detail: a far house with all its vertices pays for detail nobody can see. Pick a mode, change one thing, and watch the bars against their budgets.

The Reef's Coral Garden, mid-range phone. Goal: 30 fps and a render thread of at most 12 ms.

          What this shows
          
    
          
      
          A draw call is CPU paperwork
          Each slip costs the render thread about 10 µs, and each material switch 20 µs more, however small the mesh. "Free on the GPU" can still be 42.5 ms on the CPU.
          
      
          Share first, then merge
          renderer.material made 1,200 copies. Sharing the materials cut the switches from 1,250 to 56. Only then can instancing merge 1,200 fish into 6 draws.
          
      
          Every merge has a price
          Static batching saves 2.6 ms for 18 MB. Instancing costs 1 µs per fish and moves culling to you. Neither removes a single triangle: at 4,000 fish the GPU is the limit again.
          
    
          
    
          Lesson 025 · Overdraw
          
    
          
      
          Pixels, not particles
          The 10 foam puffs at the lens are 4% of the particles and 69% of the painted pixels. Halving 200 bubbles saves 1.6 ms; capping 10 puffs saves much more.
          
      
          See-through layers are paid in full
          Opaque pixels can be skipped by the depth test. Every see-through layer runs the shader and does a read-mix-write: here 1 ms of pixel work and 4 ms of memory work per screen.
          
      
          The fill budget is shared
          Before the splash, the Coral Garden has room for 0.5 screens. Fading the murk during the close-up frees 2.5, so a 2.0-screen splash fits at 27 ms.
          
    
          
    
          Lesson 027 · Culling
          
    
          
      
          The GPU culls too late
          The GPU throws away off-screen triangles only after their vertex shader has run. 2,820 props nobody could see cost 14.1 ms of vertex work and 2.82 ms of render thread. Culling on the CPU stops them before they cost anything.
          
      
          Each kind of culling catches a different "unseen"
          Frustum: outside the view. Occlusion: behind a house. HLOD: so far away that 50 houses can be one simple mesh. In the street, occlusion does the work. At the lighthouse, only HLOD helps.
          
      
          Culling has a bill too
          Occlusion costs 2.24 ms of main thread and 3.2 MB in the street; at the lighthouse with HLOD it costs 0.54 ms and hides nothing. HLOD costs 21.6 MB. Check every camera, not one.
          
    
          
    
          Lesson 028 · Level of detail
          
    
          
      
          Pay for the pixels, not the model
          A house 180 m away is 77 px tall but still sends 2,400 vertices: about 3 pixels per vertex. 82% of the ferry view's vertices went to objects beyond 120 m.
          
      
          Every level has a floor
          House LODs cut the houses from 15 to 5.04 ms. The pines' last mesh LOD still needs 600 vertices to look like a pine; a 4-vertex impostor card takes 2,900 far pines from 21.75 ms to 0.145 ms, for 1.45 ms of pixel work and 2.7 MB.
          
      
          LOD bias is a quality dial
          The low-end phone does vertices 3× slower, and a lower resolution doesn't help vertices. Bias 0.5 brings its GPU from 34.99 to 25.28 ms. Cross-fades hide pops but draw both meshes while they fade.
          
    
          
    
          This lab is a simulation, not a measurement. Costs come from Lesson 024: render thread = 2 ms + 0.01 ms per draw call + 0.02 ms per material switch + 0.001 ms per instanced fish; main thread 14 ms; GPU = 3 ms + the larger of the pixel load (24 ms) and the vertex load (6 ms + 3 ms per 400 fish); the game caps at 30 fps. Real per-draw costs depend on the graphics API, the driver and the engine (Vulkan and Metal are cheaper per draw than OpenGL ES), and real engines also spend main-thread time on culling and sorting each object.
          
    
          The Overdraw mode is also a simulation, using Lesson 025's numbers: 1 screen = 1600 × 720 = 1,152,000 painted pixels; each see-through screen costs 1 ms in the pixel station and 4 ms in the output & memory station (2 ms with the 4-byte format); scene loads before see-through layers: vertex 15, raster 5.3, pixel 22, memory 15 ms; GPU = 3 ms + the slowest station. The heatmap is a sketch of the same 240 particles; its colours are not measured. On real phone GPUs, blending happens in on-chip tile memory and costs differ per vendor.
          
    
          The Culling mode is also a simulation, using Lesson 027's numbers: render thread = 2 ms + 10 µs per draw + 20 µs per material switch (50, plus 1 per HLOD cluster) + 1 µs per instanced prop; GPU vertex load = 2 ms + 0.03 ms per house + 0.005 ms per prop + 0.15 ms per HLOD cluster; pixel 20, memory 18, raster 4 ms; GPU = 3 ms + the slowest station; main thread = 12 ms + 0.2 µs per frustum test + (occlusion) 0.5 ms + 1 µs per occlusion test. The map is a sketch: which objects are hidden was chosen to match the lesson's counts, not computed from real sight lines. Real engines cull on worker threads, and the cost per test depends on the engine.
          
    
          The LOD mode is also a simulation, using Lesson 028's numbers: the ferry camera sees 500 houses (15–250 m) and 3,000 pines (60–450 m), spread evenly inside each distance band; the map uses a square-root distance scale. Vertex load = all vertices ÷ 80,000 per ms (3× slower on the low-end phone), with 320,000 vertices (4 ms) for everything else; pixel = 20 ms + 0.5 µs per impostor; GPU = 3 ms + the slowest station; render thread = 2 ms + 10 µs per draw + 20 µs per material + 1 µs per pine instance (2× on the low-end phone); main thread 14 ms (28 ms on the low-end phone). A cross-fading object draws both meshes; the extra dithered pixel work is not counted. Pixels per vertex use a 12 × 10 m box for a house and a 6 × 15 m triangle for a pine on a 1600 × 720 screen with a 60° view. Real LOD selection and costs differ per engine and GPU.
